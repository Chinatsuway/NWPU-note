\subsection{Towards Living Polymerisation}

As long as “living” polymer is kept tightly closed (no \ce{H2O}, \ce{ROH}, \ce{CO2}, \ce{O2}) more monomer can be added and the molecular weight will increase.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item MW increases steadily with time/conversion
    \item Narrow PDI($<1.2$ or $1.3$)
    \item Can add more monomer or another monomer/blocks.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{Kinetic Issue}

To make FRP "living", need to inhibit termination and chain-transfer without affecting initiation/propagation too much.

\subsubsection*{Strategy 1: Reversible Termination}

Also, Reversible Deactivation.

$$
\sim P_n \cdot + X \cdot \ce{<=>[k_d][k_a]} \sim P_n -X \qquad (\text{"Dormant(sleep) FR"})
$$

\textbf{Note:} Do not use Strategy 1, either refer to specific chemistry (NMP, or TEMPO) or "reversible deactivation"

\subsubsection*{Strategy 2: Catalytic/Assisted Reversible Termination}

$$
\sim P_n \cdot + X-Y \cdot \ce{<=>[k_d][k_a]} \sim P_n -X +Y\qquad (\text{"Dormant(sleep) FR"})
$$

X can be single atom transferred to $P\cdot$, Y normally a transition metal.

Also, \textbf{Catalytic Reversible Deactivation.}

\subsubsection*{Reversible Chain-Transfer(Degenerative Transfer)}

$$
\sim P_n \cdot + \sim P_m-X \ce{<=>[k_d][k_a]} \sim P_n-X + \sim P_m \cdot
$$

In processes $\sim P_n \cdot$ or $\sim P_m \cdot$ themselves, \textbf{Termination still occurs}.

\begin{mynote_env}[frametitle=To be effective]
    Reversible Termination should be $k_d > k_a$\\
    Reversible Chain-Transfer is $k_d \sim  k_a$
\end{mynote_env}

\textbf{Disadvantages:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficult to replicate literature results
    \item Very sensitive to \ce{O2} (will oxidise \ce{Cu+} $\rightarrow$ \ce{Cu++}, stopping activation)

\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{ARGET-ATRP(Activators Re-Generated by Electron Transfer)}

Using external reducing agents to convert \ce{Cu++} $\rightarrow$ \ce{Cu+}. So, more tolerant of \ce{O2} and low quantity of \ce{Cu}.

\subsubsection*{SARA-ATRP(Supplement Activation Reducing Agent)}

Addition of \ce{Cu(I)} to both reduce \ce{Cu++} $\rightarrow$ \ce{Cu+}, and active \ce{C-Br/C-Cl} to \ce{C*(FR)}.

\section{Non-Vinyl Hyperbranched Polymer}

\subsection{Branching in Step-Growth}

\subsubsection*{Carothers for linear system}

\begin{equation*}
    \overline{DP} = \frac{1+r}{1+r-2rp}
\end{equation*}

\subsubsection*{Carothers for branched system}

\begin{equation*}
    \overline{DP} = \frac{2}{2-p f_{av}}
\end{equation*}

$f_{av}$: Functional factor, average number of functional groups per monomer, in linear chain: $=2$.

\begin{equation*}
    f_{av} = \frac{N_A f_A+N_b f_B+ \cdots +N_i f_i}{N_A+N_B+ \cdots +N_i}
\end{equation*}

{\noindent
$N_A$:number of moles of monomer A,\\
$f_A$:functionality of monomer A.}

In Ring-Opening: only branching, no crosslinking, so added functional group has only 1 functionality.

\subsection{Gel Point}

At \textbf{Gel Point}, the system becomes a gel:
a crosslinked polymer.

gels are soft solids swollen with a liquid, in this particular case, just crosslinked and also presence of solvent or liquid monomer softens the material. Higher crosslinking and removal of solvent hardens the material.

\begin{equation*}
    p_G = \frac{2}{f_{av}}
\end{equation*}

$p_G$: conversion at gel point.

\begin{mynote_env}[frametitle=Note]
    Carothers theory overestimates gel point(experimental < calculated $p_G$).
\end{mynote_env}

\subsubsection*{Hyperbranched Polymers vs Dendrimers}

\textbf{Hyperbranched Polymers:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item[$\checkmark$] ``One-pot'' reactions (reactions that lead to desired product in just one reaction)
    \item[$\checkmark$] Intermediate properties (viscosity)
    \item[$\times$] Large polydispersity
    \item[$\times$] Not so good control over end-groups and branching
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Dendrimers:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item[$\checkmark$] Monodisperse polymers with defined branching and end-groups
    \item[$\checkmark$] Excellent/extreme properties (viscosity)
    \item[$\checkmark$] Excellent carriers (cavities or pockets)
    \item[$\times$] Very difficult/costly synthesis over a large series of reactions
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Characterisation of Branching}

\subsubsection*{Mark-Houwink-Sakurada Equation}

\[
[\eta ] = KM^\alpha
\]

Where,
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $[\eta ]$: Intrinsic viscosity.
    \item $M$: Molecular weight of the polymer
    \item $K,a$: Constants that depend on solvent, temperature and type of polymer.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Star Polymers}

Many polymer "arms" from a central "core" forming a star shaped polymer, called \textbf{Star Polymers}.

\subsubsection*{Miktoarm}

"Mixed" arms, which means different types of arms onto a core.

\subsection{Synthesis Methods of Star Polymers}

\subsubsection*{Core First}

Begin from central multi-functional initiator.

Using RDRP(or CRP):

\begin{enumerate}
    \item No termination
    \item No chain transfer
    \item Fast initiation and controlled propagation(to make sure all arms have similar length).
    \item Enable block copolymer arms.
\end{enumerate}

However:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Low number of arms per star molecules(3-8)
    \item No Miktoarm
    \item Can no design arms separately.
\end{enumerate}
