\section{Introduction of Rubber \& Elastomer}

\subsubsection*{Definition}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Elastomer}, A macromolecular material which returns to its initial shape rapidly after a weak stress.
    \item \textbf{Rubber}, A \textbf{insoluble} but can \textbf{swell} elastomer, and can not easily reshaped by heat and pressure.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Polyisoprene}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hevea NR},\textit{cis}-1,4-polyisoprene
    \item \textbf{Gutta or Percha NR},\textit{trans}-1,4-polyisoprene
\end{itemize}

Hevea NR has advantages:

\begin{itemize}
    \item $T_f=130^\circ C$,$T_{d-set}=200^\circ C$, and $T_{d-max}=270^\circ C$.
    \item NR contains small amounts of fatty acids and proteinaceous residues that promote sulphur vulcanisation and possibly strain induced crystallisation. \textbf{Self-reinforcing effect}.
    \item Has Excellent properties, including processing, mechanical, gas permeability and electron insualation.
\end{itemize}

However, Hevea NR is \textbf{Unsaturated \& Non-polar}, easy to be degraded.

\subsubsection*{Epoxidized Natural Rubber, ENR}

Epoxidized natural rubber (ENR), a chemically modified derivative of NR, can be easily and efficiently produced through \textit{in-situ} epoxidation of the NR latex.

\subsection{Rubber Types}

\subsubsection*{Polybutadiene Rubber, BR}

A conjugated diene monomer

\fastfigure{BR}

\textbf{Ziegler-Natta BR}, High \textit{cis}-isomer concentration, it mimics $100\%$ \textit{cis}-1,4 structure of NR.

\subsubsection*{Polychloroprene, CR}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \ce{C-Cl} dipoles enhance interchain interaction and crystallization
    \item CR has good adhesion to polar substrates
    \item The electron-withdrawing \ce{Cl} atom protect the double bond from \ce{O2} and \ce{O3}.
\end{itemize}

\fastfigure{CR}

Applications include wire, cable, hose, and some mechanical goods.

\subsubsection*{Styrene-Butadiene Rubber}

Also name SBR, a copolymer of styrene and butadiene.

\fastfigure{SBR}

SBR is the most widely used synthetic elastomer.

SBR is synthesised via free-radical as an emulsion(High $T$ cause highly branched, so often in \textit{cold} or $5^\circ C$) or anionically in solution.

It does not strain crystallise. Therefore reinforcing fillers such as \textit{silica} or \textit{carbon black} have to be used to impart adequate properties

\subsubsection*{Acrylonitrile-Butadiene Rubber, NBR}

Polarity in NBR is introduced by the polar monomer, acrylonitrile, excellent fuel and oil resistance.

With increased acrylonitrile content, there is an increase in Tg, reduction in resilience, lower die swell, decreased gas permeability, increased heat resistance, and increased strength.

\fastfigure{NBR}

\begin{mynote_env}[frametitle={Higher ACN Ratio}]
    \textbf{Lower} crosslink density, \textbf{Larger} compression set, \textbf{Higher} glass transition temperature $T_g$.
\end{mynote_env}

\subsubsection*{Ethylene-Propylene Rubber, EPR or EPDM}

EPRs and EPDMs have excellent resistance to weathering and good heat stability. Excellent electronic insulation and Elasticity(only less than NR and BR).

But:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Slow cross-linking
    \item Low tact and adhesion
    \item Low crystalline and self-reinforcement
\end{enumerate}

Applications include roofing, seals, gaskets, and hose, widely in aerospace.

\subsection{Entropic Elasticity of Rubber}

Unlike metals and ceramics whose elasticity derives from \textbf{internal energy} changes (bond stretching/bending), rubber elasticity is primarily \textbf{entropic} in nature.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Thermodynamic basis}: $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$. For an ideal elastomer, $\Delta H \approx 0$, so the retractive force arises entirely from the entropy term $-T\Delta S$.
    \item \textbf{Molecular origin}: Polymer chains in the unstretched state adopt random-coil conformations with \textbf{maximum conformational entropy}. Stretching reduces the number of accessible conformations, lowering entropy ($\Delta S < 0$).
    \item \textbf{Retractive force}: $f = \left( \frac{\partial G}{\partial L} \right)_T = -T \left( \frac{\partial S}{\partial L} \right)_T$. The restoring force is \textbf{proportional to absolute temperature} $T$ — opposite to metals, where stiffness decreases on heating.
    \item \textbf{Gough--Joule effect}: A stretched rubber band \textbf{contracts} when heated (and expands when cooled), directly demonstrating the entropic origin of elasticity.
    \item \textbf{Requirements for entropic elasticity}:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Long, flexible polymer chains (high molecular weight)
        \item Light cross-linking to prevent viscous flow (network structure)
        \item $T > T_g$ (chains must be mobile above the glass transition)
    \end{enumerate}
\end{itemize}

\section{Chemistry \& Physics of Synthetic Rubbers}

\subsection{Vulcanizing/Curing Mechanism}

\fastfigure{Vulcanizing}

\subsubsection*{Accelerators}

Accelerator increase the speed of vulcanization and to permit vulcanization to proceed at \textbf{lower temperature} and with \textbf{greater efficiency}.

Accelerator also Decreases the Quantity of Sulphur necessary for vulcanization and thus improving 'aged' properties of the rubber vulcanizates.

The use of secondary accelerators increases the speed of vulcanization substantially but at the expense of scorch safety. The dosages of the secondary accelerators are generally between $10-40\%$ of the primary accelerator.

\subsubsection*{Activators}

Metal oxide(main) and fatty acid.

Activate cure and improve the efficiency of sulfur based cure systems.

\subsection{Rubber Mixing}

To improve the performance and cost saving, \textbf{Various Agents} should be added into rubber.

\textbf{Rubber mixing} is mix the agents and rubber by mechanical force.

\subsection{Plastication of Rubber}

Increasing plasticity of rubber by mechanical or chemical method to meet the various processing requirements of rubber.

Including cold plastication, heat plasticization, and \textbf{peptizer (or plasticizer)}.

\subsubsection*{Mechanical Plastication}

The probability of breaking molecular chain is $\propto $ mechanical worked, and inversely $\propto $ the temperature of rubber.

However, the free radicals can re-combine the chain rapidly, so only mechanical force is not enough.

\subsubsection*{Oxygen}

\ce{O2} can degrade free radicals, and react with rubber to make them degradation.

\begin{mynote_env}[frametitle={Cold and Heat Plasticization}]
    In cold plasticization, the breaking of chain is mainly caused by mechanical force.\\
    In heat plasticization, the main reason is oxygen degradation.
\end{mynote_env}

\subsection{Reinforcement}

Particulate fillers can increase the strength of an amorphous rubber. The filler should have high specific surface area.

\subsubsection*{Carbon Black}

Primary structure of carbon black, about $300\, nm$.

Secondary structure, the space network formed by van der Waals force. It should be crushed by mechanical force.

\begin{mynote_env}[frametitle={Oil adsorption}]
    Crude but effective method to determine how much oil is required to fill up structural voids in carbon black aggregates. Units are $㎝ ^3·g^{-1}$.\\
    The higher oil adsorption, the higher structure of CB.
\end{mynote_env}

\subsection{Anti-Degradants}

\textbf{Aromatic Amines}, Chemical antiozonants are required to protect all of the external vulcanized rubber components from ozone-induced cracking.

\section{Characterization}

\subsection{Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA)}

TGA measures amount and rate of weight change vs. temperature or time in a controlled atmosphere.

Characterizes materials that exhibit weight loss or gain due to decomposition, oxidation, or dehydration

\begin{mynote_env}[frametitle={Cautions}]
    TGA are all kinetic processes, which means it dependent on absolute temperature and time spent at that temperature.
\end{mynote_env}

\subsection{Differential Scanning Calorimetry(DSC)}

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) measures the energy absorbed(endothermic) or produced (exothermic) as a function of time or temperature.

\fastfigure{DSC}

It is used to characterize melting, crystallization, resin curing, loss of solvents, and other processes involving an energy change.

DSC may also be applied to processes involving a change in heat capacity, such as the glass transition

\begin{mynote_env}[frametitle={Cold Crystallization}]
    When a polymer is rapidly cooled (quenched) from the melt to below $T_g$, chains are frozen in an amorphous state.\\
    Upon reheating above $T_g$, chains gain mobility and reorganize into crystalline regions — this exothermic peak is \textbf{cold crystallization} ($T_{cc}$).\\
    It occurs between $T_g$ and $T_m$, indicating the polymer was not fully crystallized during cooling.
\end{mynote_env}

\section{Structure \& Property of Rubber}

\subsection{Statistical Elasticity Theory}

Consider a single polymer chain at equilibrium:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Random coil
    \item $n$ segments of length $l$
    \item Total contour length is $nl$
    \item The distance between chain ends is $r$
\end{itemize}

The chain position:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Freely Jointed Chain}, $(\bar{r^2})^{\frac{1}{2}}=n^{\frac{1}{2}}l$
    \item \textbf{Freely Rotating Chain}, $(\bar{r^2})^{\frac{1}{2}}=(2n)^{\frac{1}{2}}l$
\end{itemize}

From statistical thermodynamics, entropy

$$
S = k \ln \Omega
$$

Then,

$$
S=c-k b^2 r^2 \; b^2=\frac{3}{2nl^2}
$$

\subsection{Neo-Hookean Stored Energy Function}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stored energy function} (incompressible):
    $$
    W = \frac{\mu}{2}(I_1 - 3), \quad I_1 = \lambda_1^2 + \lambda_2^2 + \lambda_3^2
    $$
    where $\lambda_i$ are principal stretch ratios, $I_1$ is the first invariant of the Cauchy--Green deformation tensor.
    \item \textbf{Shear modulus}: $\mu = nkT = \frac{\rho RT}{M_c}$, where $n$ is chain density, $k$ Boltzmann constant, $T$ absolute temperature, $\rho$ density, $R$ gas constant, $M_c$ molecular weight between crosslinks.
    \item \textbf{Incompressibility constraint}: $\lambda_1\lambda_2\lambda_3 = 1$, rubber deforms at constant volume.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Hyperelastic Models}

Hyperelastic means:

\begin{itemize}
    \item No loss of energy
    \item No time dependence of the behaviour
    \item No path dependence on final stress and strains.
\end{itemize}

Hyperelastic models include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Neo-Hookean}: $W = \frac{G}{2}(I_1 - 3)$. Simple one-parameter model; good for small/medium strains but poor at large strains.
    \item \textbf{Mooney--Rivlin}: $W = C_{10}(I_1 - 3) + C_{01}(I_2 - 3)$, where $I_2 = \lambda_1^{-2}\lambda_2^{-2} + \lambda_2^{-2}\lambda_3^{-2} + \lambda_3^{-2}\lambda_1^{-2}$. Better than Neo-Hookean for larger strains.
    \item \textbf{Rivlin series}: $W = \sum_{i,j=0}^{\infty} C_{ij}(I_1 - 3)^i(I_2 - 3)^j$.
    \item \textbf{Arruda--Boyce}: accounts for finite chain extensibility; parameters $G$ and $\lambda_m$.
    \item \textbf{Gent}: $W = -\frac{G}{2}(I_m - 3)\ln\left(1 - \frac{I_1 - 3}{I_m - 3}\right)$; captures strain hardening.
    \item \textbf{Yeoh}: $W = C_{10}(I_1 - 3) + C_{20}(I_1 - 3)^2 + C_{30}(I_1 - 3)^3$; only $I_1$ needed.
\end{itemize}

\begin{mynote_env}[frametitle={Model Selection}]
    If possible use a strain-energy function of $I_1$ only. Then a single characterization test is sufficient.
\end{mynote_env}

\subsubsection*{Uniaxial Tension}

For incompressible uniaxial tension ($\lambda_1 = \lambda$, $\lambda_2 = \lambda_3 = \lambda^{-1/2}$):

\begin{itemize}
    \item Neo-Hookean engineering stress: $\sigma = G(\lambda - \lambda^{-2})$.
    \item True stress: $t = \sigma\lambda = G\left(\lambda^2 - \lambda^{-1}\right)$.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Limitations of Neo-Hookean Model}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Medium strains}: network imperfections (loose ends, closed loops, trapped entanglements) cause slight overestimation.
    \item \textbf{High strains}: Gaussian assumption fails; finite extensibility and strain-induced crystallisation become important.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Inelastic Behaviour}

Real rubber is not always hyperelastic. Inelasticity arises from:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Viscoelastic effects (temperature and rate dependence, proximity to $T_g$).
    \item Presence of reinforcing fillers.
\end{enumerate}

Consequences:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Time/rate dependence
    \item Path dependence
    \item Energy loss (hysteresis)
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Glass Transition}

$T_g$ is not a true thermodynamic transition; it is a change in the timescale needed for chain segments to reach equilibrium. Fast deformation at high $T$ is equivalent to slow deformation at low $T$.

\subsubsection*{Dynamic Mechanical Analysis}

A sinusoidal deformation gives:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Storage modulus $E'$: elastic component.
    \item Loss modulus $E''$: viscous component.
    \item Loss factor $\tan\delta = E''/E'$: peaks around $T_g$.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Time--Temperature Superposition (TTS)}

WLF equation shifts isotherms along the frequency axis to produce a master curve:

\[
\log a_T = \frac{-C_1(T - T_{ref})}{C_2 + (T - T_{ref})}
\]

Typical universal constants: $C_1 = 17.4$, $C_2 = 51.6$ (when $T_{ref} = T_g$).

\subsubsection*{Static Inelasticity}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Creep}: deformation under constant stress. Creep rate tracks $\tan\delta(T,\omega)$.
    \item \textbf{Stress relaxation}: stress decay under constant strain. Physical relaxation (network reorientation) dominates initially; chemical relaxation (bond scission) dominates at high $T$/long times.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Mullins Effect}

Cyclic stress softening in filled rubber. The equilibrium curve depends on the maximum previously applied strain. Softening can recover over time in the unstrained state.

\subsubsection*{Payne Effect}

Dynamic modulus decreases with increasing strain amplitude in filled rubber. Mechanisms include filler-network breakdown/reformation, polymer adsorption/desorption at filler surface, and disentanglement.

\subsection{Failure: Aging and Swelling}

Rubber properties change in service due to microstructural changes:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Thermal aging
    \item Ozone/oxygen attack
    \item Swelling by liquids
    \item Gas permeation and chemical attack
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Thermal Aging}

High temperature causes:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Continued curing (if under-cured) $\rightarrow$ stiffer.
    \item Network modification: crosslink scission/interchange/insertion, main-chain scission, extra-network material.
\end{enumerate}

Bond energies: C--C ($\sim 352$ kJ/mol) strongest; polysulfidic C--S$_x$--C ($\sim 268$ kJ/mol) weakest. Interestingly, weaker crosslinks can yield stronger vulcanisates through yielding and reformation.

\subsubsection*{Ozone/Oxygen Ageing}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Diffusion-controlled; mainly affects surface layers.
    \item Ozone cracking occurs only under tensile strain.
    \item Protected by antioxidants and waxes that bloom to the surface.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Swelling}

Crosslinked rubber swells when solvent mixing free energy overcomes the elastic resistance of the network. Equilibrium is described by the Flory--Rehner equation:

\[
\ln(1 - v_r) + v_r + \chi v_r^2 + \nu_e V_1 v_r^{1/3} = 0
\]

where $v_r$ is the volume fraction of rubber, $\chi$ the polymer--solvent interaction parameter, $\nu_e$ the effective crosslink density, and $V_1$ the solvent molar volume.

Solubility parameter $\delta$: when polymer and solvent $\delta$ are close, swelling is strong.

\begin{mynote_env}[frametitle={Effect of Filler}]
    Filler does not swell and strongly interacts with polymer, so true crosslink density cannot be calculated directly from swelling experiments of filled compounds.
\end{mynote_env}

\subsubsection*{Gas Saturation and Chemical Attack}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Gas solubility follows Henry's law: $c = sP$.
    \item \ce{H2S} can crosslink HNBR, increasing modulus and $T_g$ while reducing elongation.
    \item Lifetime prediction by accelerated kinetics is complicated because $T$ affects reaction, solubility and diffusion simultaneously.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Strength of Rubber}

Rubber usually fails by crack growth from flaws, not by uniform tensile rupture. The crack-growth characteristics determine strength.

\subsubsection*{Fracture Energy}

Energy balance for crack growth:

\[
\delta W \ge \delta U_{el} + G_c t\,\delta a
\]

$G_c$ (or $T$) is the energy absorbed per unit crack area (J m$^{-2}$). It is a material property independent of specimen geometry.

\subsubsection*{Tearing Tests}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Pure shear: $T = W h$ ($W$ = strain-energy density, $h$ = height).
    \item Trouser tear: $T = \frac{2\lambda F}{t} - w_0 W$.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Factors Affecting Strength}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Viscoelasticity: more viscoelastic rubber generally has higher $G_c$.
    \item Crystallisation: NR strain-crystallises at the crack tip, blunting cracks; less rate/temperature sensitive.
    \item Fillers: increase hysteresis and cause crack branching/blunting.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Void Inflation and Rapid Gas Decompression}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Hydrostatic tension causes cavitation.
    \item For a spherical void in Neo-Hookean material, critical inflation pressure $P_c = \frac{5G}{2}$.
    \item High initial modulus is therefore desirable for RGD-resistant seals.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Friction of Rubber}

Rubber friction does not follow Coulomb's law ($\mu = F/N$ constant). It depends on:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Surface roughness of rubber and substrate
    \item Adhesion
    \item Viscoelastic properties (temperature, structure, filler)
    \item Lubricants and humidity
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Friction Components}

\[
F_{total} = F_{adhesion} + F_{deformation} + F_{viscous} + F_{cohesion}
\]

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Adhesion}: dominates on smooth dry surfaces; involves stick-slip and Schallamach waves.
    \item \textbf{Deformation (hysteresis)}: dominates on rough surfaces; cyclic deformation of rubber by asperities.
    \item \textbf{Viscous}: due to fluid films, reduces friction.
    \item \textbf{Cohesion}: from tearing rubber.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Pressure and Velocity Dependence}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Friction coefficient decreases with increasing normal pressure because the real contact area grows sub-linearly.
    \item Velocity and temperature dependence can be described by WLF time--temperature superposition.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Wet Friction and Hydroplaning}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Water can reduce friction by 20--30\%.
    \item A thick water film causes hydroplaning, lifting the rubber from the substrate.
    \item On ice, the surface is near its melting point, making friction behaviour very sensitive to pressure, speed and temperature.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Tyre Tread Design}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Good wet grip: high $\tan\delta$ at frequencies corresponding to road roughness.
    \item Low rolling resistance: low $\tan\delta$ at frequencies corresponding to tyre rotation.
\end{itemize}
